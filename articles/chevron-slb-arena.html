Chevron joins a 10-year AI subsurface deal by seating its experts inside SLB

SLB, TotalEnergies and now Chevron are building reservoir software together. More than two years in, the new contribution is people in the building, and no one has published a result.

LW

LatentWire Staff

Oct 18, 2026 · 8:00 AM ET · 4 min read

SLB and TotalEnergies have spent more than two years co-developing digital tools for reservoir engineering. On October 6, Chevron joined them. What Chevron is sending, along with money and intellectual property, is its own subsurface experts, working inside SLB's technology centers.

The collaboration is called Arena. Chevron Technical Center, a division of Chevron U.S.A. Inc., is the second operator in a partnership SLB and TotalEnergies signed on July 2, 2024, for 10 years. That original deal already named the method. Subsurface modeling and interpretation would be "accelerated and automated using AI and real-world learnings from both companies."

Chevron's version of those learnings is specific. Jitendra Kikani, Chevron's vice president of subsurface, said the company is "combining our world-class expertise in uncertainty analysis with digital technologies and AI-enabled workflows to improve decision quality, accelerate insights, and enhance operational efficiency." The expertise he named is uncertainty analysis, optimization and field development planning. Those are judgments. They are not a data feed.

What the announcement actually says

SLB's October 6 release says Chevron will contribute investment, intellectual property and technical expertise, and will help shape the roadmap for the digital tools. Rakesh Jaggi, president of SLB's digital business, said the collaboration "brings SLB's digital and subsurface expertise together with operator knowledge." Chevron, he said, adds experience that will "help strengthen how we address complex subsurface workflows and field development decisions."

The sentence that describes how Arena runs is the one about seating. By embedding TotalEnergies and Chevron subject matter experts inside SLB technology centers, the companies say they will shorten the usual feedback loop between operators and product teams. SLB says that produces "scalable, open and extensible" digital technologies.

Arena also points at older joint work. SLB says the companies are building on the collaboration that delivered the Intersect reservoir simulator.

Why seating the expert is the point

A reservoir model is only as useful as the decision someone will make with it. Uncertainty analysis and field development planning are where that decision sits. They depend on which analog an asset team trusts, which failure it has already seen, and which number it will not let the software smooth over.

That knowledge does not arrive as a clean table. The 2024 partnership already said the work would use "real-world learnings," not AI alone. Two years later, the way those learnings enter the product is still a person from the operator, sitting with the people who build the tool.

Kikani's line about AI-enabled workflows is a claim about direction. The announcement does not say Arena has cut a cycle time, raised a recovery factor, or retired a handoff someone was doing by hand. It says the experts are going into the building so the feedback loop gets shorter.

The scarce input in this deal is not another model. It is the person who already knows which answer is wrong.

Ten years, and no price

The term is the only hard number attached to the deal. On July 2, 2024, SLB and TotalEnergies signed for 10 years, starting with reservoir engineering and geoscience modeling on SLB's Delfi platform. The same release also covered geological carbon storage. Namita Shah, president of OneTech at TotalEnergies, said the modeling tools should help the company "reduce emissions" and make progress on that storage work.

Chevron has not said what it is paying, what intellectual property it is contributing, or how many people it is sending. SLB has not either. A 10-year framework can hold a large investment or a small one. From the outside those look identical.

The order is the part other companies can see. The platform came first. The second operator arrived 27 months and 4 days later, in the form of experts. Buying a license and expecting the workflow to be inside it runs those steps the other way around.

The lesson for everyone else

A lot of oil and gas AI work starts with a platform and a pilot. Arena starts from the other end of the same problem. The workflow that decides a well, a workover or a development plan still lives with the people who do it. Until someone writes that down with them, the software is automating the official process, which is often not the one the asset team follows.

The smaller version does not require a 10-year co-development. Pick one recurring job. Talk to the people who did it last week, not the owner of the process. Find the handoff that still happens by hand. That is the step worth software. The rest is a license.

What we still don't know

This is an announcement, not a result. There is no figure for Chevron's investment, no headcount of embedded experts, and no before-and-after on decision time, drilling outcomes or production. It is not clear whether any Arena software is in use on a Chevron asset, or when it would be.

The 2024 line about automating modeling with AI is still a plan. Jaggi said then that the partnership would "benefit the whole industry." The October release does not say which operators outside the three companies can use what Arena builds, or on what terms. Intersect shows that these companies can ship a simulator together. It does not show that Arena has.

This story draws on reporting by the Journal of Petroleum Technology, published October 15, 2026, and on SLB's announcements of October 6, 2026 and July 2, 2024.
