PE put AI on diligence. Only 7% of portfolio companies run it at enterprise scale

Ninety-five percent of AI initiatives meet the business case. Most of that case is still the front of the deal. Value creation and exit are thinner.

LW

LatentWire Staff

Oct 19, 2026 · 8:00 AM ET · 4 min read

Private equity has put AI on the part of the deal that already produces documents. Diligence rooms get read faster. Portfolio companies that actually change how they operate are still the exception.

That is the picture John Sviokla lays out in a Forbes piece syndicated on Yahoo Finance on October 16. Deal sourcing and diligence have the tools. Value creation has pockets of results. Exit has anecdotes, not a dataset showing buyers pay more for AI-driven gains.

The gap shows up in the ownership numbers. FTI Consulting surveyed 200 fund and operating leaders. Ninety-five percent said their AI initiatives met or beat the original business case. Only 36% of portfolio companies use AI across use cases. Seven percent operate at enterprise scale. FTI notes those business cases were often conservatively scoped.

Where AI has taken hold

In an S&P Global Market Intelligence survey from February 2026, 31% of general partners said AI is somewhat or fully integrated into diligence, the highest share of any function. Firms use it to read data rooms faster. Bain's Asia-Pacific reporting also finds leading firms running diligence on what AI does to the target itself, not only on the files in the room.

Sourcing is messier. The same S&P survey found that 64% of GPs rate AI as ineffective for deal sourcing. A Bain and StepStone survey found GPs reporting their highest generative-AI returns in sourcing and diligence. Sviokla's reading is that results depend on how a firm uses the tools. He cites one firm that used Claude to go from screening about 10 deals a month to screening every deal presented to them.

The hold period is thinner

Ownership is where the return math lives, and where the surveys get less generous. Bain and StepStone found that portfolio benefits skew toward cost savings, and that 39% of GPs expect no material financial impact from AI in 2026. Meeting a conservatively scoped business case is not the same as changing a company's operating leverage.

That matters because of Bain's arithmetic, as Sviokla cites it. A buyout now needs roughly 12% annual EBITDA growth for the return that 5% delivered a decade ago. Faster IC memos do not produce that. Changing how a portco closes a ticket, forecasts demand, or runs a plant might. Seven percent of companies at enterprise scale is not a hold-period program. It is a set of pilots that still need a sponsor.

Reading the data room is not the same as changing the company you buy.

Exit still has a story, not a multiple

Sviokla writes that he found no dataset showing buyers pay more at exit for AI-driven gains. What exists is adviser anecdote from EisnerAmper. A distribution company whose AI demand forecasting lifted inventory turnover 15% moved from about 7 times EBITDA to about 9 times. A healthcare analytics company with a proprietary patient database drew a 12 times revenue multiple. A marketing agency saw buyers push for a lower multiple because AI tools could replicate much of its work.

The condition in each case is the same. Buyers distinguish AI embedded in operations from superficial use. The premium goes to the first. The discount lands on businesses AI can copy. Software buyouts already felt the other side of that. Bain's midyear report named an AI-driven rout in software valuations among three market shocks, and MSCI data in that report show buyout software marks down about 8% in the first quarter.

The lesson for everyone else

The front of the deal rewards whoever can ingest documents. The back of the hold rewards whoever can show a buyer a measured change in how the company runs. Those are different jobs. One lives in the data room. The other lives with the people who still close work in a spreadsheet, a chat thread, or a night-shift workaround.

Firms that treat AI as a diligence accelerator will keep reporting high "success" rates on projects that never leave the deal team. Firms that want a multiple have to start with one workflow inside a portco, talk to the people who do it, and show what stopped happening by hand. That is the evidence buyers already say they can tell from a slide.

What we still don't know

How firms define "enterprise scale" in the FTI survey. How much of the 95% success rate is cost takeout versus revenue. Whether the EisnerAmper exit anecdotes are outliers or a pattern. Whether any large sample yet links AI-driven operating metrics to a paid-up exit multiple.

Sviokla's own frame is that private equity is well placed to force the change if it invests in capability, not only tools. The surveys he cites do not yet show that happening at the back of the cycle.
